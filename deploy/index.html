A paired benchmark for route following and pedestrian-aware navigation.

Abstract

Vision-and-language navigation in human-populated spaces requires agents to ground language while responding to moving pedestrians and social-safety constraints. We present DPed-VLN, a Habitat 3.0 benchmark containing 33,093 navigation episodes with paired global and prior-augmented instructions. The benchmark combines ORCA-controlled humanoid pedestrians, socially constrained expert paths, and navigation-efficiency and pedestrian-safety metrics reported side by side. Global instructions provide ordinary route guidance, while prior-augmented instructions include dynamic-pedestrian cues for the same episodes. These conditions support comparisons of policy responses to the available language annotations. We provide DPet (Dynamic Pedestrian-aware Network) as a reference implementation trained with reinforcement learning or imitation learning. We also evaluate representative vision-and-language navigation policies under zero-shot and parameter-efficient adaptation settings.

Pedestrian representation

The benchmark renders articulated, textured humanoids controlled by ORCA, while HA-VLN 2.0 uses monochrome human meshes. The richer appearance and articulated motion reduce visual domain shift for pretrained vision-language encoders and make pedestrian interaction explicit in the dataset analysis.

Two language conditions

LEVEL 1

Global instructions

Route and destination guidance grounded in the static scene, without pedestrian-specific priors.

LEVEL 2

Prior-augmented instructions

The same route guidance enriched with concise cues about pedestrian motion, risk, and crowd context.

Supplementary analysis

The supplementary material expands the benchmark construction, instruction semantics, sensing setup, and action statistics used by the main experiments.

Instruction generation

The paired-instruction pipeline starts from the same expert episode. Level 1 uses static scene context and route-level observations for goal-oriented guidance. Level 2 receives the additional dynamic-pedestrian context extracted from the recorded trajectory, then produces prior-augmented language. Both conditions refer to the same episode, so their comparison isolates the effect of pedestrian-related guidance.

Expert action distribution

The expert trajectories contain mostly forward actions, with left and right turns forming the next largest groups. This distribution explains why the imitation objective uses inflection weighting: turns and avoidance transitions carry more behavioral information than repeated straight motion.

Instruction semantics

The word clouds show that Level 1 emphasizes static spatial expressions such as rooms, hallways, and directions. Level 2 retains the route language while adding terms related to people, avoidance, and spatial interaction, reflecting the additional pedestrian priors.

Pedestrian-related vocabulary

The keyword-frequency analysis quantifies the semantic difference between the two corpora. Pedestrian and avoidance terms occur more frequently in Level 2, consistent with the dynamic context supplied during its generation.

Instruction length distributions

Word-count distributions are reported for both train and scene-held-out validation episodes. The split distributions remain closely aligned across the two levels, indicating that the paired instruction protocol changes semantic content without introducing a large length shift.

Additional sensing experiment

This additional experiment visualizes the sensing configuration used in the navigation setup and provides a compact view of the geometric information available to the policy beyond the RGB stream.

Cross-validation view

The cross-validation matrix summarizes how the paired instruction conditions and training paradigms interact. It should be read together with the detailed tables above: the diagonal and off-diagonal entries distinguish matched evaluation from transferring a policy across instruction levels.
